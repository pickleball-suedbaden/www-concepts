Four ways the club website could look and work

Each concept is a complete landing page with the same content, the same sample sessions and the same demo features: trial booking, membership application, member login, newsletter, contact and the town vote. That way you compare the design and the order of the homepage, not missing pieces.

A, B and C are built from the logo’s colours and style. D, Same Court, is a fresh, photo-led direction.

All content is placeholder. Anything in [brackets] or marked SAMPLE is invented or still to be confirmed, and the forms send nothing. The concepts are in German and English, with French to follow; the live site will open in German, with French and English one click away.

All photos are AI-generated samples. The people in them are not club members, and each photo is tagged “AI sample” with its shot-list number. The live site will use only real photos from club sessions, taken with consent. Shots 05 (the founders) and 23 (the board) stay empty on purpose: they must be real.

How to judge

The content and features are the same in all four, so four questions are enough.

  1. Logo-led or photo-led?

    A, B and C take their colours, type and illustration style from the logo. D answers a different question: should the site lead with people and photos rather than the logo’s colours?

    D uses paper, ink and one lime accent, the colour of the ball, so the photos bring the colour. The logo appears small in the header and large once, at the bottom. Settle this first, because it narrows the choice.

  2. Which one feels like your club?

    Picture showing it to a curious newcomer, a sponsor and the town council. Look past the placeholder text and judge the mood: the type, the colours and each concept’s signature moment, such as A’s passport stamp, B’s name tag, C’s 60-second court or D’s eight-to-eighty row of players.

  3. Which homepage order fits year one?

    Each concept puts something different first:

    • Try-firstA. The next sessions lead, for people ready to book. It works best once there is a regular schedule.
    • Story-firstB. The people and the First 100 lead, with sessions right after. We recommend this for the launch period, before there is a regular schedule.
    • Sport-firstC. The game is explained first, for visitors who have never heard of pickleball.
    • Photo-firstD. Photos of every age group lead, with sessions straight after, for visitors who want to see people like them on court before they book.

  4. Look, order and photos can be mixed.

    You don’t have to take a concept whole. You can pick one concept’s look with another’s order, for example B’s look with A’s Try-first order once the schedule is real.

    Photos mix in too. A, B and C show AI sample photos in their photo slots, so you can judge each look with photography in it. A logo-led look could also take D’s bigger, bolder photos.

    Single pieces can move as well, such as D’s eight-to-eighty row, C’s 60-second court as the “What is pickleball?” page, or A’s passport stamps.

Try them properly: look at each concept on a phone first, since most visitors will arrive on one, then on a computer. Press “Try it free” and “Become a member”: both run through to the end and send nothing. The “Concept · placeholder content” badge in each page’s corner explains that concept.

Concept A

Paddle Passport

Open concept A: Paddle Passport →

A Südbaden travel poster for a sport. The pickleball rises like a sun over the Black Forest, the next sessions run on a station departures board, and newcomers collect stamps in a Paddle Passport before they join.

Photos
7 AI sample photos (shots 01, 03, 12, 13, 14, 15, 24) in postcard frames further down the page. The illustration still leads. See the whole page of Concept A

Strengths

  • The strongest regional story. The Black Forest poster, the Rhine and the Baden-arms paddles say “our corner of Europe” at a glance, and the poster could be printed for town halls.
  • Dates on the first screen on a computer. The departures board shows the next sessions and free spots right beside the headline. On a phone they follow the poster, and still come sooner than in any other concept.
  • Every travel object has a job. Boarding passes are the session cards on phones and the booking confirmation, passport stamps turn taster visits into a game, and fare tickets carry the fees.

Trade-offs

  • Many metaphors. Poster, board, passport, tickets and badges can tip into kitsch, and the illustration needs real design effort in production.
  • It promises a lot early. “Three countries” is a big claim for a club that starts in one town, and a departures board only shines once there is a regular schedule.

Concept B

Saved You a Spot

Open concept B: Saved You a Spot →

A warm, editorial club journal that treats every visitor as the fourth player a doubles game is waiting for, with trilingual name tags, a founders’ wall of 100 numbered spots, and the table after the last point.

Homepage order
Story-first People lead, sessions follow.
Opening sections
  1. Name-tag hero: “We saved you a spot”
  2. This week at the club (sessions)
  3. Your first evening, hour by hour
  4. Who plays here, then the First 100
Fonts
Frauncesheadlines Figtreetext and forms Caveathandwritten names and notes
Leads with
People and paper. Low motion: tags press on and underlines draw.
Photos
10 AI sample photos (shots 01, 02, 03, 17–21, 24, 26), mostly in “Your first evening” and the “Who plays here” grid. The illustrations still lead. See the whole page of Concept B

Strengths

  • The warmest welcome for nervous beginners. Type your first name and the button becomes “Save my spot, Lena →”. The name follows you into the booking and is never saved.
  • Built to recruit the First 100. The founders’ wall of numbered name tags, named hosts and the evening “after the last point” sell belonging, not only a sport.
  • Credible for sponsors and the council. The calm journal layout and the plain “Club notices” section, with the board, status lines and documents, read as serious.

Trade-offs

  • It depends on real people. The AI sample photos show how it should look, but until there are real photos and names, the journal can look thin.
  • Furthest from the logo’s sporty lettering. The serif and handwriting feel editorial, and it is the longest page of the four, especially on phones.

Concept C

Rally Mode

Open concept C: Rally Mode →

A landing page that plays like a pickleball rally: the logo ball serves itself into the hero, hops from headline to headline, teaches the game on a 60-second playable court, and lands every time on “Try it free”.

Homepage order
Sport-first The game is explained first.
Opening sections
  1. Hero with “Where do you land?” choices
  2. The 60-second playable court
  3. Sessions this week
  4. From First Serve to Founders’ Cup
Fonts
Sairaheadlines and score calls Intertext and forms
Leads with
Type and motion. High motion, on a strict budget: the travelling ball and squashing headlines.
Photos
7 AI sample photos (shots 01, 02, 03, 04, 14, 15, 26) in slanted viewfinder frames. Type and the ball still lead. See the whole page of Concept C

Strengths

  • Best at explaining the sport, which is the biggest barrier. The 60-second court teaches the serve, two bounces, the kitchen and scoring by playing.
  • “Loud line, calm line.” Headlines and the game are loud and slanted; dates, prices, forms and club pages stay plain. It is fun without losing trust.
  • Shares the logo’s energy. Heavy italic type like “PICKLEBALL”, and score calls from “0–0–2 · SERVE” to “11–0–2 · GAME” that teach scoring as you scroll. Strong for press and social media visitors.

Trade-offs

  • Lots of motion. It could read as a gimmick, it needs the most care for accessibility, and it is the most work to build and maintain.
  • Less of it shows on phones. The playable court sits behind a tap so the session dates stay near the top, and the travelling ball appears only on larger screens.

Same Court

A photo-led campaign site, and a fresh direction. Lively action photos and portraits of every generation playing together carry the page, the interface stays quiet in paper and ink, and the logo signs off rather than sets the colours.

Photos

Strengths

  • A fresh, photo-led direction. Action photos and players of every age carry the page. The logo’s yellow and red appear only inside the logo, which sits small in the header and large once at the bottom.
  • It answers “Is this for me?” at a glance. “Eight to eighty. Same court.” sits over a mixed-age rally, and the next section shows six players aged 10 to 74, each with one line on why the game works at that age.
  • Modern, yet still a club. With the photos bringing the colour, dates, fees and forms stay plain and easy to read. It looks like a sports campaign to sponsors and the council, and stays warm and local.

Trade-offs

  • It needs a real photo shoot before launch. Every section leans on photos, so the club must photograph its first sessions, with consent from adults and from parents for children. None of the AI samples can go live, while A–C still work with only a few real photos.
  • Less of the logo, more to scroll. The club’s colours appear only in the logo, so the local feel has to come from photos of the Rhine and the vineyards. It is a long page, and on phones the session dates come a little later than in A–C.
